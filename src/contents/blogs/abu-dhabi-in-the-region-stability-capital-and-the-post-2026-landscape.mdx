**THE ABU DHABI SERIES · THE ECONOMIC HISTORY AND FUTURE OF ABU DHABI**  
**Arc III: The Future, Potential and Its Constraints**

This final article draws the series together, and it does so through the hardest test the emirate has faced in a generation. Everything I have described across these ten pieces, the sovereign wealth, the diversification, the energy system, the wager on intelligence, the human-capital question, ultimately serves a single purpose: to make Abu Dhabi resilient enough to remain a regional anchor of capital and stability through whatever the region throws at it. In 2026 the region threw a great deal. I want to assess how the emirate navigated that year strictly as an economic and strategic matter, and I want to be clear from the outset that I take no position whatsoever on the politics or the rights and wrongs of the conflict itself. My subject is the economy, its resilience, and its limits, and I will hold to that.

A word on method, because the brief that governs this series demands it and the subject requires it. Every figure relating to the conflict period is date-stamped and drawn from dated, attributable sources, and where a situation remained unresolved at the time of writing in September 2026 I say so rather than imposing a false conclusion. This is a landscape still in motion, and honesty about what is not yet known is part of assessing it responsibly.

### The shock, described neutrally

The facts of the disruption are a matter of public record. According to dated reporting and research briefings, on 28 February 2026 the United States and Israel began a series of strikes against Iran, and in the retaliation that followed, shipping through the Strait of Hormuz was largely blocked from that date. The strait normally carries roughly a fifth of the world’s seaborne oil and a comparable share of its liquefied natural gas, and analyses cited by the UK House of Commons Library recorded reductions in crude and LNG shipping through it on the order of ninety per cent or more. Regional oil output was curtailed sharply at the peak of the disruption. This was, by any measure, a first-order economic shock to the Gulf, and Abu Dhabi sits directly on the water in question. I recount this only to establish the scale of the test, and I attribute rather than editorialise.

The essential point for this series is that the shock was largely exogenous. It did not arise from any failure of the emirate’s economic strategy, and it was not within Abu Dhabi’s power to prevent. That distinction matters, because it means 2026 functioned as an unplanned stress test of resilience that had been built, or not built, over the preceding decades. The interesting question is not why the shock happened, which is a political matter outside my scope, but how the economy the earlier articles described actually performed when the stress arrived.

### What the resilience investment bought

Here the years of unglamorous infrastructure spending earned their return, and this is the clearest vindication in the series of a theme I have pressed throughout: that Abu Dhabi’s real achievements are often the least photogenic ones. Faced with the closure of the strait, the emirate was able to route a substantial volume of crude through the Abu Dhabi pipeline to the Fujairah coast on the Gulf of Oman, physically bypassing the Hormuz chokepoint, as documented by the US Congressional Research Service. That pipeline, built years earlier for exactly this contingency, did more for the emirate’s economic resilience in 2026 than any strategy document, and it is a concrete illustration of what patient, long-horizon investment in resilience is actually for. The same held on the domestic side: reporting from September 2026 indicated that the emirate’s energy and water systems continued to function through the disruption without interruption to service, a quieter but equally telling demonstration of a system built to absorb stress.

The deeper resilience, though, was financial and reputational, and it connects directly to the sovereign-wealth architecture of the second article. In a region under acute stress, capital seeks stability, and Abu Dhabi has spent decades making itself the most stable destination for capital in its neighbourhood. Its vast sovereign reserves, its low fiscal breakeven, its diversified income and its reputation for continuity meant that, even as conflict disrupted the region around it, the emirate remained a relative safe harbour. That is the economic meaning of the phrase regional anchor: not that Abu Dhabi is untouched by regional turmoil, but that it is more able than its neighbours to absorb turmoil and continue functioning, and in 2026 that capacity was tested and, in important respects, held.

This safe-harbour role has an active dimension as well as a passive one, and it connects to a theme from the article on sovereign wealth. Abu Dhabi does not merely hold capital defensively; it deploys it as an instrument of relationships, including through large, multi-year investment frameworks with partner economies. In a fractured region, capital of this scale buys more than financial return: it buys relevance, access and a place at tables where the emirate’s security and prosperity are discussed. That is a genuine source of resilience. It is also, as the year showed, not a guarantee, because relevance secured through capital still depends on partners choosing to act on it, and in 2026 the limits of that leverage were on display alongside its reach. Capital buys a hearing; it does not buy an outcome.

### The limits the year exposed

Balance, which has governed every article in this series, requires me to be equally clear that 2026 also exposed the limits of the emirate’s strategy, and I would be failing the standard I set myself if I described only the resilience and not the vulnerability. The first and most fundamental limit is geographic. Abu Dhabi can build bypass pipelines and diversify its trade routes, but it cannot move itself off the Gulf, and it remains, by location, exposed to instability in one of the world’s most contested waterways. The bypass infrastructure reduces the dependence on the strait; it does not eliminate the emirate’s physical presence in a volatile neighbourhood. No amount of capital repeals geography, and 2026 was a reminder that the emirate’s prosperity is built next to a chokepoint it does not control.

The second limit concerns the strategy of security through great-power ties, and here I will describe the tension neutrally without endorsing any view of it. Abu Dhabi has, over many years, pursued resilience partly by binding major powers closely to its interests through economic, technological and security relationships. The events of 2026 prompted a visible reassessment, discussed openly by regional analysts, of how much protection such ties actually provide when a crisis arrives, since the emirate found itself exposed to a disruption that its relationships did not prevent. Whatever one concludes from that, the strategic lesson is economic as much as political: a resilience strategy that relies heavily on the choices of external powers is only as reliable as those powers’ alignment with the emirate’s interests at the moment of stress, and that alignment cannot be assumed.

The third limit is the one the artificial-intelligence article foreshadowed, and 2026 made it concrete. In July 2026, dated reporting indicated that the United States eased AI-related export restrictions on the UAE, with the emirate’s cooperation during the year cited as a factor. I note this strictly as a documented economic development, not as a political judgement. But it illustrates a structural reality this series has returned to repeatedly: the emirate’s access to the technology on which its future economy depends is entangled with its geopolitical relationships, and benefits and costs in that domain move together. Resilience in one dimension can deepen dependency in another, and an honest account holds both.

One further feature of the emirate’s conduct in 2026 is worth recording neutrally, because its logic is economic even where it looks political. Through the disruption, Abu Dhabi worked to keep channels of communication open across the region’s divides rather than closing them, a posture consistent with a state whose prosperity depends on trade, capital flows and stability rather than on any single alignment. Whatever one makes of the diplomacy itself, its economic rationale is straightforward: an economy this open to the world has a direct material interest in de-escalation and in remaining a place that all sides can still do business with. Pragmatism, in this reading, is not a sentiment but a balance-sheet strategy, and it is of a piece with everything else this series has described about how the emirate protects what it has built.

### The synthesis: what the series adds up to

Let me now draw the whole series into a single assessment, because that is what a final article owes the reader. Across these ten pieces I have tried to describe an emirate that is neither the effortless success of the promotional literature nor the fragile petro-state of the sceptics, but something more interesting and more real: a place engaged in a serious, partially successful, still-unfinished attempt to convert a finite resource into a durable, diversified and resilient economy, and doing so with unusual discipline and unusual self-awareness. The pearling collapse taught it what dependence on one thing costs. The sovereign funds are its answer. The diversification is real but incomplete, the energy transition genuine but bounded by physics, the intelligence economy ambitious but dependent, the human-capital question unsolved, and the regional position strong but exposed.

What holds these threads together is a single strategic project: resilience. Every institution and every bet this series has examined is, at bottom, an attempt to make the emirate able to withstand shocks, whether the slow shock of the energy transition or the sudden shock of a regional conflict. Seen through that lens, 2026 was not a detour from the story but its central test, and the verdict is genuinely two-sided. The resilience Abu Dhabi built proved real: the bypass held, the services continued, the capital stayed, the state functioned. And the year also proved that resilience has limits that capital cannot buy past: geography, great-power dependency, and exposure to a region whose stability the emirate influences but does not command. Both halves of that verdict are true, and the maturity of the emirate’s position lies in the fact that its own strategists appear to understand this better than its cheerleaders do.

For an investor or a policy reader, the useful conclusion is a calibrated one. Abu Dhabi is among the most stable, best-capitalised and most strategically coherent economies in its region, and its resilience is not a marketing claim but a tested reality. It is also an economy whose long-term ambitions run into genuine constraints, on human capital, on technological dependency, on physics, and on geography, that are real, structural and unresolved, and that will still be there when the current crises have passed. The right posture toward the emirate is neither the boosterism that ignores the constraints nor the cynicism that ignores the achievements, but the clear-eyed respect owed to a serious project that is doing difficult things seriously, and that knows, better than most, what it has not yet solved.

That is where I will leave this series, and where the author’s wider work on the region begins. Abu Dhabi is best understood not as a finished model to be admired or dismissed, but as an ongoing answer to a question every resource economy eventually faces: how to build something that outlasts the resource. The emirate has been working on that answer since a pearl-diving coast lost its single industry nearly a century ago, and it is working on it still. The events of 2026 tested how far it has come. The honest reading is that it has come a very long way, that the distance still to travel is real, and that the discipline with which it faces that distance is, in the end, the most credible thing about it. I have tried throughout to assess rather than to celebrate, because assessment is the higher compliment, and this is an emirate that has earned the harder look.
