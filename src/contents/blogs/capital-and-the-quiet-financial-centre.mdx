**THE ABU DHABI SERIES · THE ECONOMIC HISTORY AND FUTURE OF ABU DHABI**
**Arc II: The Present Economy, Assessed Rather Than Celebrated**

There is a version of this story that writes itself, and I want to avoid it. In that version, Abu Dhabi’s financial centre, ADGM, is the ambitious challenger, Dubai’s DIFC is the incumbent, and the only question is when the challenger overtakes the champion. It is a tidy narrative, it flatters Abu Dhabi, and it is largely wrong. The two centres are not running the same race. They were built on different logic, they serve overlapping but distinct constituencies, and the interesting analytical question is not who wins but why each exists in the form it does. This article resists the temptation to declare a victor, because declaring one would mean misunderstanding both.

### What ADGM has actually achieved

The growth is real and worth stating precisely, from the centre’s own reporting. ADGM, established in 2015 on Al Maryah Island and since extended to Al Reem Island, marked its tenth year in 2025 with assets under management up 36 per cent for the year, active licences crossing 12,000, and 347 financial institutions in residence by year end. The first half of 2025 alone saw assets under management rise 42 per cent and more than 1,800 new licences issued, and ADGM described itself as the largest international financial centre in the MENA region by the market capitalisation of its listed registered entities. The roster of firms that established or expanded there, spanning global asset managers, banks, and digital-asset players, is genuine, and in December 2025 a major crypto exchange secured a formal licence from its regulator. These are not vanity metrics. They reflect real institutions choosing to book real business in Abu Dhabi.

The texture of that activity is worth a moment, because it reveals what kind of centre ADGM is becoming. The institutions establishing there in recent periods have included global asset managers and banks alongside a striking concentration of alternative-finance and digital-asset players, and several of the emirate’s largest sovereign-linked ventures, private credit platforms, reinsurance vehicles and technology-investment structures, have been built through it. This is not the profile of a general-purpose banking centre. It is the profile of a hub oriented toward asset management, alternatives and financial innovation, tightly coupled to large pools of institutional and sovereign capital. That specialisation is a deliberate positioning choice, and it plays directly to the advantage the emirate actually possesses rather than to one it would have to manufacture.

Underneath the numbers sits a distinctive design worth understanding. ADGM applies English common law directly, rather than through a codified local adaptation, which gives international institutions and their lawyers an unusually familiar legal environment. It built one of the earliest comprehensive regulatory frameworks for digital assets, positioning itself deliberately at the innovative edge of finance. And, most importantly, it sits inside the gravitational field of the largest concentration of sovereign capital in the region. When the emirate’s own funds and their partners structure vehicles, private credit platforms, reinsurance ventures and technology investments, a great deal of that activity is booked through ADGM. Its proximity to sovereign and institutional capital is its single greatest structural advantage.

Two further strands of ADGM’s strategy deserve mention, because they show a centre trying to lead rather than follow. Its early and relatively comprehensive regulation of digital assets has made it a destination for crypto and fintech firms seeking a credible, rules-based jurisdiction, culminating in the licensing of a major global exchange, a position that carries both real opportunity and the obvious risks that attach to a fast-moving and lightly-tested asset class. Separately, the centre has pursued a role in the internationalisation of the Chinese currency and in building financial links eastward, reflecting Abu Dhabi’s broader tilt toward Asian capital and trade. Both are bets on where finance is heading rather than where it has been, and both suit a centre whose comparative advantage is agility rather than incumbency. They may not all pay off, but they are coherent with the centre’s chosen character.

### What DIFC still holds

Balance requires that I describe the incumbent with the same care, and here the celebratory Abu Dhabi narrative tends to go quiet. Dubai’s DIFC remains the larger and more established centre by most conventional measures, with a deeper and broader base of financial institutions built over a longer period: hundreds of banking and capital-markets firms, a very large population of wealth and asset managers including a substantial hedge-fund community, and a mature ecosystem of insurers, law firms, auditors and the dense professional infrastructure that a financial centre accretes only over time. That depth is not easily or quickly replicated, and it gives DIFC advantages in liquidity, network effects and the sheer availability of expertise that ADGM, for all its momentum, has not yet matched.

It is also worth being honest about the arithmetic of growth rates. A centre growing assets under management by 36 or 42 per cent is expanding impressively, but often from a smaller base, and high percentage growth off a younger, smaller centre is not the same as surpassing a larger, older one in absolute terms. I have seen the challenger framing lean heavily on growth rates while quietly omitting the base they grow from. A fair account keeps both in view: ADGM is growing faster, and DIFC remains larger and deeper. Neither of those facts cancels the other, and a reader who holds only one of them has been given half the picture.

> A centre growing by forty per cent from a smaller base is not the same as one surpassing a larger, older rival. Growth rate and scale are different measurements, and the challenger story confuses them.

### Two centres, two logics

Once you set the two side by side honestly, the more illuminating question emerges, which is why they differ rather than which is ahead. DIFC grew up as Dubai’s general-purpose international financial centre, serving the trade, commerce and diverse capital flows of an economy built on openness and movement, and it accumulated the broad, deep institutional base that such a role produces. ADGM grew up in a different economy, one defined by sovereign capital, and its character reflects that origin: closer to the institutional allocators, family offices and sovereign entities of Abu Dhabi, more concentrated on asset and wealth management and on regulatory innovation, and more tightly woven into the emirate’s own investment ecosystem. The two centres are, in a real sense, expressions of the two emirates I contrasted in the first article of this series.

It is also worth situating both centres honestly against the wider world, since neither the challenger nor the incumbent yet rivals the truly global hubs. London, New York, Singapore and Hong Kong command a depth of liquidity, a breadth of instruments and a density of expertise built over generations, and both UAE centres remain, for now, regionally dominant and globally emerging rather than globally central. This is not a criticism; it is a matter of stage. But it is the proper frame for the ADGM-versus-DIFC question, which can otherwise take on an inflated significance. The two are competing, productively, for leadership of a region whose collective financial weight is still climbing toward the global tier, not for a throne that either has yet reached.

This is why the honest verdict is that they compete in some areas, complement in others, and each leads where its underlying logic gives it an edge. They compete for internationally mobile asset managers, for fund domiciliation, for the firms that could plausibly locate in either. They complement each other as parts of a single national financial offering, giving international capital two credible, common-law, English-language jurisdictions within one country, which is itself a competitive advantage for the UAE against financial centres elsewhere. And each leads on its own turf: DIFC on breadth, depth and maturity; ADGM on proximity to sovereign capital and on regulatory agility at the frontier of digital and alternative finance. Declaring an overall winner would require ignoring the dimension on which the other leads.

### The constraint ADGM must be honest about

Even-handedness cuts toward Abu Dhabi as well, so let me name the constraint that the challenger narrative prefers to leave out. A significant portion of ADGM’s activity is connected, directly or indirectly, to the emirate’s own sovereign wealth ecosystem. That proximity is its greatest strength, but it is also a form of concentration. A financial centre whose growth is substantially fuelled by the state’s own capital and the partners of that capital is, to a degree, growing on internal demand rather than purely on independent international choice. This is not disqualifying, and every young centre relies on anchor clients. But it means that some of ADGM’s impressive growth is the financial-sector reflection of the same sovereign machine I examined earlier, rather than fully independent evidence of the centre winning open competition on its own merits.

The maturity gap is the other honest constraint. Depth in a financial centre is partly a function of time: the accumulation of institutions, talent, precedent, liquidity and trust that cannot be bought quickly at any price. ADGM has compressed a great deal of development into a decade, which is genuinely impressive, but a decade is still a decade against DIFC’s longer run and against the century-scale histories of the global centres both aspire to rival. Recognising that gap is not a slight. It is simply the difference between momentum and incumbency, and both are real assets of different kinds.

Talent is the quiet variable underneath all of this, and it connects to a structural theme this series returns to. A financial centre runs on people: lawyers, fund managers, compliance specialists, overlaying compliance specialists, auditors and the deep professional bench that takes years to assemble. Both UAE centres draw that talent overwhelmingly from abroad, and their continued growth depends on remaining places where international professionals actively choose to live and work. That reliance is a strength in the short run, giving instant access to global expertise, and a dependency in the long run, tying each centre’s fortunes to its ongoing attractiveness against rival hubs and to the cost of operating there. It is, in effect, the financial-sector version of the human-capital question that sits beneath the whole diversification project, and it constrains ADGM and DIFC alike.

### The assessment, without a scoreboard

My conclusion is deliberately not a ranking. ADGM is a genuine success, growing quickly, distinctively designed, and strategically positioned at the intersection of sovereign capital and financial innovation, and it has become a serious international centre in a remarkably short time. DIFC remains larger, deeper and more mature, with advantages in breadth and network that time alone confers and that ADGM has not yet matched. The two are best understood not as rivals with one finish line but as two financial centres built on the two different economic logics of the two emirates, competing at the margins, complementing at the core, and together giving the UAE a double-anchored financial offering that few countries can match.

For an investor or a policy reader, the useful takeaway is to stop asking which will win and start asking which fits a given purpose, because that is the question the structure was actually built to answer. Sovereign-adjacent capital, alternative assets and regulatory-frontier activity gravitate naturally toward ADGM; broad, mature, general-purpose financial business toward DIFC. The emirate did not build ADGM to defeat Dubai. It built a financial centre that expresses Abu Dhabi’s particular character as a place of sovereign capital, and on those terms, and measured against that intention rather than against a borrowed scoreboard, it has largely succeeded. That is the fair account, and it is more interesting than the horse race it is usually mistaken for.
