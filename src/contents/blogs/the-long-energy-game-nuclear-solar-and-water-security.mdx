**THE ABU DHABI SERIES · THE ECONOMIC HISTORY AND FUTURE OF ABU DHABI**  
**Arc III: The Future, Potential and Its Constraints**

The fourth article in this series examined Abu Dhabi as a producer and exporter of hydrocarbons. This one turns the question inward and asks something more elemental: can the emirate power and water itself, sustainably and securely, over the long run? It is a less glamorous subject than oil diplomacy or artificial intelligence, and it is more fundamental than either, because an economy that cannot keep the lights on and the taps running through a hot, dry century has no future to diversify into. I find this the most clarifying subject in the series precisely because it is where strategy stops being a matter of capital and ambition and becomes a matter of physics, engineering and resource reality.

### The nuclear achievement, stated plainly

Begin with the part of the story that is a genuine and largely delivered success, because there is one, and even-handedness requires acknowledging it as clearly as any constraint. The Barakah Nuclear Energy Plant, in the Al Dhafra region, is the first nuclear power station in the Arab world and now one of the larger nuclear facilities commissioned anywhere in the past decade. Its four Korean-built APR-1400 reactors, developed by the Emirates Nuclear Energy Corporation and brought online in sequence between 2021 and 2024, provide a total capacity of about 5.6 gigawatts and generate on the order of 40 terawatt-hours a year, enough to supply roughly a quarter of the UAE’s electricity. On the figures reported by the operator, it avoids something like 22 million tonnes of carbon dioxide annually.

What makes Barakah strategically important is not only its scale but its character. Nuclear provides stable, around-the-clock baseload power at a lifecycle carbon intensity comparable to wind, and it does so from a single site rather than across the tens of square kilometres that equivalent firm solar capacity would require. In a system that is adding large amounts of intermittent solar, that steady baseload is what keeps the grid reliable when the sun is not shining. It also does real work for the wider decarbonisation effort: a large share of Abu Dhabi’s clean-energy certificates, which heavy industries buy to certify lower-carbon aluminium and steel, are backed by Barakah’s output. This was a difficult thing to build well, and the emirate built it well and fast. That deserves to be said without hedging.

### Solar, and the limits of the sun

The renewable side of the ledger is similarly real. Through Masdar, established in 2006, and projects such as the 1.2-gigawatt Noor Abu Dhabi and the 2-gigawatt Al Dhafra plants, the emirate has built some of the larger and lower-cost solar installations in the world, and Masdar carries an ambition to reach 100 gigawatts of renewable capacity globally by 2030. Abu Dhabi has reported clean-power capacity rising toward 8.8 gigawatts and clean sources supplying more than half of the emirate’s electricity by 2025, up sharply from a small share only a few years earlier. Sunlight is one resource the desert has in abundance, and the emirate has learned to convert it cheaply.

But the sun has a hard limit that no amount of ambition removes: it does not shine at night, and solar output is intermittent by nature. A grid cannot run on solar alone; it needs storage, baseload, or both. This is precisely why the nuclear baseload matters, and why Masdar is now building large solar-and-battery systems designed to deliver round-the-clock power. The honest framing is that solar is a powerful and economical part of the answer, not the whole of it, and that the real engineering challenge is not generating clean electrons in the daytime, which the emirate has largely solved, but delivering firm, reliable clean power around the clock, which is harder and more expensive and still a work in progress.

It is worth being concrete about what firming intermittent power actually costs, because the economics are part of the honesty. Daytime solar in the Gulf is now genuinely cheap, among the lowest-priced electricity ever contracted anywhere. Around-the-clock clean power is not, because it requires either expensive battery storage, dispatchable backup, or baseload nuclear whose upfront cost is very large even where its lifetime cost is competitive. The headline figures for cheap solar and the reality of firm, reliable clean supply are therefore different numbers, and conflating them overstates how far the problem is solved. Abu Dhabi has the capital to build firm clean capacity, and it is doing so, but it is buying reliability at a real premium over the cheap daytime electrons. That premium is the true cost of a low-carbon grid in a desert, and it is easy to miss when only the record-low solar prices are quoted.

### The demand that keeps rising

Here I have to introduce a complication that the celebratory accounts of clean-energy progress usually leave out, because it changes the whole picture. The clean-power build is not happening against static demand. It is happening against demand that is rising fast, from several directions at once. Extreme heat means enormous and growing cooling loads. Population and industry keep expanding. And, as the article on artificial intelligence described, the emirate is deliberately building gigawatts of new data-centre capacity that will consume very large amounts of electricity. Adding clean supply while demand climbs this quickly means that much of the new clean capacity is not decarbonising the existing system so much as keeping pace with new load. Running to stand still is still real progress, but it is a different and more modest claim than simply replacing fossil generation, and the distinction matters.

> The clean-energy build is not racing against a fixed target. It is racing against demand that keeps rising, from cooling, from industry, and now from the gigawatts that artificial intelligence consumes.

### Water: the unglamorous foundation

If nuclear is the achievement and solar the workhorse, water is the constraint that underlies everything and receives the least attention, which is exactly why I want to give it the most. The UAE is among the most water-scarce countries on earth. It has no meaningful rivers, little rainfall and limited and stressed groundwater, and it depends for most of its fresh water on desalination, the energy-intensive process of taking the salt out of seawater. This is not a peripheral technical matter. It is the physical foundation on which the entire population and economy sit. Every ambition in this series, every resident, every industry, every data centre, ultimately depends on the emirate’s ability to manufacture fresh water from the sea, indefinitely, in a warming climate.

The engineering here is genuinely serious, and the emirate is making a consequential transition. Gulf desalination has historically been thermal, boiling seawater using waste heat from gas-fired power plants, which ties water production to fossil generation. Abu Dhabi is shifting toward reverse osmosis, which pushes seawater through membranes and can run on electricity from any source, including nuclear and solar. The Department of Energy has set a goal of using reverse osmosis for nearly all the emirate’s desalination by 2035, up from around a quarter in 2022. Decoupling water production from fossil fuels in this way is one of the more important and least celebrated moves in the entire clean-energy strategy, because it addresses the foundation rather than the facade.

There is an elegant forward possibility here that the operator has signalled interest in: pairing nuclear energy directly with desalination, potentially through small modular reactors, so that the same clean, steady heat and power that anchor the grid also manufacture the water. If it were delivered at scale, it would tie the emirate’s two hardest resource problems, firm clean power and fresh water, into a single solution. I flag it as a genuine prospect rather than an accomplishment, because it remains largely at the study stage, and the gap between an attractive concept and a delivered system is exactly the kind of distance this series is careful not to collapse. But it points to the seriousness with which the emirate is thinking about the underlying physics, rather than only about the headline capacity figures.

Even so, the constraints are permanent and worth stating soberly. Desalination remains energy-intensive, which means water security and energy security are bound tightly together; it produces concentrated brine that must be managed responsibly in an already-stressed sea; and it makes the population structurally dependent on a manufactured resource with limited natural buffer. Reverse osmosis and clean power reduce the carbon cost of this dependence, but they do not remove the dependence itself. A desert economy that must desalinate the sea to drink is running a permanent engineering commitment, not solving a problem once. This is the clearest example in the whole series of ambition meeting physics: no strategy, however well-capitalised, repeals the fact that the land is dry and the sea is salt.

### The tension that will not resolve

There is one further tension I must carry over from the article on ADNOC, because it applies with equal force here and honesty forbids leaving it out. The UAE has committed to a national net-zero target by 2050, and the domestic clean-energy build described above is genuine progress toward the emissions produced within its borders. But the emirate remains, as earlier articles established, a large exporter of hydrocarbons whose product generates emissions when burned elsewhere. The clean domestic energy system and the hydrocarbon export economy coexist, and the net-zero framing applies principally to the former. This is not hypocrisy so much as the structural position of a producer state trying to decarbonise its own operations while continuing to sell the very fuel whose combustion it is decarbonising away from at home. I name it not to resolve it, because it does not resolve, but because a chapter on the energy game that omitted it would be incomplete.

A final observation on dependency, to keep the account balanced on all sides. The clean-energy system, like the artificial-intelligence strategy, rests partly on foreign technology and supply chains: the reactors are Korean, the fuel supply is being diversified through Western suppliers, and much of the solar and battery technology is imported. This is normal for a country building advanced infrastructure quickly, and it is not a criticism. But it means the emirate’s energy resilience, like its compute resilience, is not fully self-contained, and that is a fair thing to keep in view when assessing how secure the long game really is.

### The game measured in decades

My assessment of the long energy game is, on balance, the most positive in this arc of the series, with the constraints stated honestly rather than hidden. Abu Dhabi has done genuinely impressive and largely successful work: a world-class nuclear plant delivered on time, some of the cheapest solar anywhere, a serious move to decarbonise its water supply, and a coherent strategy to firm intermittent renewables with baseload and storage. On the domestic decarbonisation of power and water, the emirate is among the more advanced places in the world, and it earned that position through execution rather than announcement.

The honest qualifications are three, and they are permanent rather than passing. Demand is rising fast enough, driven partly by the emirate’s own ambitions in cooling and compute, that clean supply is often keeping pace rather than replacing; water security is a permanent engineering dependency that clean power softens but never removes; and the domestic net-zero effort sits alongside a continuing hydrocarbon export economy whose emissions leave with the product. This is where ambition meets physics, and physics does not negotiate. The emirate is playing the long energy game about as well as a hot, dry, capital-rich state can, and the game has no final whistle, only the steady, unglamorous discipline of powering and watering a desert, decade after decade, against constraints that do not go away. On the evidence so far, it is playing that game with unusual seriousness. Whether seriousness is enough against physics is the one question no one can yet answer.
