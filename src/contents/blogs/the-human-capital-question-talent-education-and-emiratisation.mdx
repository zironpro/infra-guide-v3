**THE ABU DHABI SERIES · THE ECONOMIC HISTORY AND FUTURE OF ABU DHABI**  
**Arc III: The Future, Potential and Its Constraints**

This is the article the series has been pointing toward since its earliest pages, and the one that tests most directly whether I am willing to discuss what is difficult rather than only what is promising. Every ambition I have described so far, the diversification, the energy transition, the wager on artificial intelligence, ultimately rests on people: on the workforce that must build and run the new economy. And here Abu Dhabi, like the wider UAE, faces a structural constraint that is more fundamental than any single policy and that no amount of capital resolves quickly. I will try to describe it plainly, because describing it plainly is the point.

### The demographic fact

Begin with the numbers, which are stark and worth stating without euphemism. The UAE’s population is estimated at around 11.4 million, of whom roughly 88 per cent are foreign nationals. The workforce mirrors this: on common estimates about 85 per cent expatriate and 15 per cent Emirati, and within that, most Emiratis have historically worked in the public sector rather than the private one. In other words, the economy that these articles have been assessing was very largely built, and is very largely run, by people who are not citizens and who, under current arrangements, mostly cannot become citizens. That is the demographic fact beneath everything else, and it cuts in two directions at once.

On one hand, this openness to global labour is a genuine strength and a deliberate achievement. The ability to attract workers and expertise from around the world, quickly and at every skill level, is precisely what allowed Abu Dhabi to build a modern economy in decades rather than generations. Few countries have made themselves as frictionlessly able to import talent, and the recent expansion of long-term residency through golden visas has extended that openness at the higher end. This is not a bug in the model; for a small-population, capital-rich state, importing human capital is a rational and effective strategy, and it has worked.

On the other hand, a workforce that is overwhelmingly expatriate and substantially transient poses a problem that openness alone cannot solve. Human capital, in the deep sense, is not only the skills a person has today but the accumulated knowledge, institutions and social trust that build up when people put down roots across a lifetime and a generation. An economy whose workers largely expect to leave, and whose skills largely leave with them, finds it harder to accumulate that deeper stock. The UAE has begun to address this at the margins with longer residency, but the structural feature remains: a great deal of the emirate’s human capital is, in effect, leased rather than owned, and can depart when the terms change.

Recent reforms have tried to soften this, and fairness requires acknowledging them. Long-term residency schemes, including the golden visa, now let some highly skilled expatriates and their families remain for extended periods without the old requirement to be tied to a single employer, which does allow more of them to put down deeper roots. This is a genuine and sensible adjustment, and it improves the emirate’s ability to retain the talent it attracts. But it stops short of a path to citizenship for the great majority, which means the fundamental character of the workforce, highly capable and structurally impermanent, is softened rather than transformed. The emirate has chosen to deepen residency without broadening nationality, and that choice, whatever its merits, keeps the deeper human-capital question open rather than closing it.

### Emiratisation, and the tension inside it

The policy response to the other half of the equation, the place of citizens in the private economy, is Emiratisation, pursued most concretely through the federal Nafis programme launched in 2021 as part of the Projects of the 50. Its instruments are clear and enforced. Nafis committed 24 billion dirhams to draw Emiratis into private-sector work, set a target of raising Emiratis to 10 per cent of skilled private-sector jobs, and imposed mandatory quotas: companies with 50 or more staff must increase their share of skilled Emirati employees by 2 per cent a year, with higher rates in sectors such as banking and finance, and firms that fall short pay a monthly financial contribution for each unfilled position. By mid-2025, official figures reported more than 152,000 Emiratis employed across some 29,000 private-sector companies, a genuine increase.

I want to assess this even-handedly, because it is easy to caricature in either direction. The case for Emiratisation is strong and legitimate. A state cannot indefinitely accept a situation in which its own citizens are largely absent from the private economy that its policies and its wealth have built. There are sound reasons of social cohesion, long-term stability and simple national ownership for wanting Emiratis meaningfully present across the economy rather than concentrated in government. The goal is not protectionism for its own sake; it is the reasonable desire of a nation to have its own people participate in its own economic life. On that level the policy is not only defensible but necessary.

It is worth understanding why the problem exists at all, because the reason shapes how hard it is to solve. For decades the public sector offered citizens secure, well-paid and comfortable employment, funded ultimately by hydrocarbon revenue, while the private sector was built around competitively-priced expatriate labour. Given that arrangement, it was entirely rational for Emiratis to prefer government work and rational for private employers to prefer expatriates. Emiratisation is, in effect, an attempt to override two decades of rational individual choices through policy, and that is inherently difficult. One is not correcting an aberration; one is working against incentives that the structure of the economy created and, in the public-sector case, still partly sustains. Any honest assessment has to reckon with the depth of the pattern being reversed, not merely with the targets set against it.

The difficulty lies in the mechanism, and honesty requires naming it. Quotas backed by fines are a blunt instrument, and blunt instruments produce blunt responses. The same authorities that run the programme have reported significant numbers of fake or artificial hirings, arrangements designed to satisfy the quota on paper without creating genuine, productive employment, and have flagged large numbers of companies for non-compliant practices. This is exactly the failure mode a quota system invites: it can manufacture compliance without manufacturing capability. When a target is enforced by penalty, the cheapest way to meet it is not always to build real skills and real roles, and some firms will take the cheapest way. The headline number of Emiratis in private employment is real, but a fraction of it reflects box-ticking rather than value creation, and no one who looks closely disputes that.

> A quota enforced by penalty can manufacture compliance without manufacturing capability. The honest question is not how many Emiratis are hired, but how many are genuinely productive.

There is a related tension around subsidy. Nafis makes private-sector work more attractive to citizens partly through salary support and other incentives, narrowing the long-standing gap between generous public-sector packages and private-sector pay. This is pragmatic and probably necessary in the near term. But it raises the same circularity question that ran through the diversification article: if Emirati participation in the private economy is sustained substantially by state support, then it is, to that degree, another form of the oil-funded state underwriting activity that does not yet fully stand on its own. The measure of success is not how many citizens are drawn into private roles with public help, but how many of those roles become genuinely competitive and self-sustaining over time. That is a harder number, and it is the one that actually matters.

### The pipeline, and the real prize

Beneath both the expatriate question and the Emiratisation question sits education, which is where the constraint is either eventually resolved or permanently reproduced. Abu Dhabi has invested seriously here, and the investment is visible: Khalifa University, the local campuses of institutions such as NYU Abu Dhabi and Sorbonne Abu Dhabi, and, pointedly, the Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence, established in 2019 as a graduate institution dedicated entirely to the field on which the emirate is betting its future. The intent is coherent, which is to build a pipeline that connects Emiratis to the specific new economy the state is trying to create, rather than educating them for an economy that no longer needs them.

The honest assessment is that this pipeline is improving but young, and that its ultimate test is not enrolment or the prestige of the institutions but outcomes: whether it produces Emiratis with skills the private economy genuinely competes to hire, at wages that need no subsidy, in numbers that shift the structural picture. That is a decades-long undertaking, and it cannot be accelerated by fiat. Building world-class universities is expensive but achievable with capital; building a generation that flows from them into productive private-sector careers and reshapes the demographic balance of the workforce is slower, harder and only partly within any government’s control. It is the real prize, and it is not yet won.

I would add one further observation that the optimistic accounts tend to omit. The two halves of the human-capital problem are in some tension with each other. The more successfully the emirate attracts world-class expatriate talent to run its most advanced sectors, the higher the bar that Emirati workers must clear to compete for those same roles on merit. Openness to global talent and the development of national talent are both necessary, but they are not automatically complementary, and managing the relationship between them, ensuring that Emiratisation raises genuine capability rather than simply reserving positions, is one of the more delicate policy problems the emirate faces. It has not been solved, and pretending otherwise would be exactly the flattery this series was written to avoid.

### The constraint beneath the ambition

So let me state the verdict without softening it. Human capital is, in my judgement, the single most underappreciated constraint on Abu Dhabi’s long-term ambitions, precisely because it is the one that capital cannot quickly buy away. The emirate can fund a data centre, a museum or a nuclear plant with a decision and a cheque. It cannot fund, on the same timescale, a self-sustaining national workforce or the deep social accumulation of a rooted population, because those are built by time and lived experience, not by allocation. Every diversification target, every technology bet, every ambition in this series eventually runs into the question of who, exactly, will build and run it, and whether that answer is durable.

None of this is a prediction of failure, and I want to be as clear about that as about the difficulty. The UAE has repeatedly solved problems that observers judged intractable, and its openness to talent gives it a genuine advantage in the near and medium term even as the deeper question remains open. But the deeper question is real, it is structural, and it will still be here when the current programmes have run their course. An economy built substantially by people who mostly cannot stay, seeking to draw its own citizens into that economy through quotas and subsidy while building an education pipeline whose results lie years ahead, is an economy doing serious and necessary work on its hardest problem. Whether it succeeds is genuinely uncertain, and saying so is not pessimism. It is the honesty that the more confident chapters of this story depend on to be believed.
